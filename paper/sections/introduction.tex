\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Around the Mediterranean, groundwater is the reserve that carries cities and farms through dry
years, and droughts and abstraction now lower it together \citep{Costa2025Five}. In the Roussillon
plain of southern France the aquifer, mostly its deep Pliocene part, supplies the drinking water,
while irrigation draws mostly on the rivers through gravity canals; in 2022--2024 the plain's
groundwater reached historic lows. Deciding who may pump, where and when, needs models that do two things:
simulate the head of each well from its drivers, and say how much of a change each driver caused.

Data-driven models now simulate groundwater levels from weather and abstraction: recurrent networks,
also trained on many wells at once \citep{Wunsch2021Groundwater,Chidepudi2025Training}, and
spatio-temporal graph networks that link neighbouring wells
\citep{Bai2023Graph,Taccari2024Spatial,Wu2025Forecasting}, beside transfer-function models
calibrated well by well \citep{Collenteur2019Pastas}. Two weaknesses remain. First,
what such a model learns about a driver is rarely checked: the static attributes of a well can act as
mere identifiers \citep{Heudorfer2024On}, and explanations of groundwater models, mostly by feature
attribution, are rarely confronted with physical knowledge \citep{Clark2025Explainable,
Zounematkermani2026Explainable}. Second, pumping is the driver that matters most for decisions and
the one known least: in the Roussillon, abstraction is declared once a year, boreholes below a
threshold are not declared, and the weekly timing has to be reconstructed, so that within a year it
moves with the weather. Interpretability and physical consistency are among the gaps that reviews
of deep learning in hydrogeology name \citep{Dai2025Incorporating}.

Knowledge-guided machine learning offers a way in: prior knowledge can shape a model's inputs, its
structure, its loss or its output \citep{Karpatne2017Theorya,Vonrueden2021Informeda,
Willard2022Integrating,Xu2024Physics}, and differentiable hybrids keep process structure inside a
learned model \citep{Shen2023Differentiable,Feng2022Differentiable}. In hydrogeology much of that
knowledge is not an equation but a set of facts and qualified statements: which unit a well
monitors, which unit a facility draws from, which units touch, where a river feeds an aquifer, and
in which direction a head responds to each driver. Such knowledge lives in databases and reports,
with uneven evidence, and ontologies and knowledge graphs are built to hold it with its provenance
\citep{Brodaric2018Enabling,Janowicz2022Know,Lippolis2025The}. Graph learners for groundwater already
combine several kinds of adjacency \citep{Wu2025Forecasting} or embed hydrogeological units as prior
information \citep{Wu2025Graph}; heterogeneous graphs model river floods \citep{Sarkar2025Hydrogat},
and a knowledge graph has been joined to a time-series foundation model in river hydraulics
\citep{Holmberg2026Knowledge}. What is missing is a model whose graph and constraints are generated
from a formal, checkable store of hydrogeological knowledge, together with a test of whether the
model uses them as intended.

Adding knowledge does not by itself make a model right for the right reasons
\citep{Ross2017Righta}. A model can reach the same accuracy through the wrong concepts, a reasoning
shortcut \citep{Marconato2023Not,Marconato2024Bears}, and an explanation can be unfaithful to the
model it explains \citep{Azzolin2025Reconsidering}. Two questions are therefore usually left open:
whether a knowledge-guided model uses its knowledge as intended, and whether a gain comes from the
knowledge or from the formalism that carries it. The second matters because an ontology adds
traceability, consistency and reuse, but the same edges could often be computed from plain tables.

This study asks when explicit hydrogeological knowledge improves groundwater simulation and the
reliability of its explanations, under uncertain pumping information. We build an ontology network,
LULC-Water, and from it a knowledge graph of the Roussillon multilayer aquifer that holds, for every
candidate driver of every monitoring well, its evidence and the direction of the head's response.
The graph supplies the inputs, the links and the sign constraints of a hybrid model, a sum of
response functions fitted for all wells at once. Four research questions follow. RQ1: does knowledge
from the graph improve simulation, as information and as structure? RQ2: do sign constraints derived
from the ontology improve plausibility and skill out of distribution? RQ3: does the model use the
knowledge, or does it take shortcuts? RQ4: can its explanations be stated in the ontology's terms and
remain faithful?

The contributions are four.
\begin{enumerate}
\item A knowledge graph of an aquifer system, built from a network of reused ontologies, in which
  every derived influence carries its evidence, confidence and eligibility as a model constraint,
  with competency questions turned into tests (\cref{sec:kg}).
\item A ladder of models that adds one kind of knowledge at a time to the same differentiable
  response model, so that information, structure and constraints are credited separately, with
  controls that keep a graph's structure or locality and remove its meaning (\cref{sec:models}).
\item A semi-synthetic twin of the aquifer that maps where routing by an imperfect graph can pay at
  all, and calibrates the tests of wrong reasons before any real-data contrast is scored
  (\cref{sec:twin}).
\item Pre-registered contrasts on real data, with an equivalence test that separates the value of
  the knowledge from the value of the ontology that carries it (\cref{sec:kg-export}).
\end{enumerate}
