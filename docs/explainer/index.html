The water under the Roussillon

What we did, what we chose, and what comes next in the project, in simple words, with pictures made from our own data.

Go down the borehole

    Chapter 1

    The question

    We want a computer that follows the underground water level week by week, and does it for the right reasons.

    Why it matters. From 2022 to 2025 the Roussillon had a record . The water in the wells fell lower than ever measured, and towns put limits on water use. Two users drink from the same underground water: the taps of the towns and the farms. When there is not enough, they compete. That competition is the story behind the project.

    What the computer does. Every week, we give it the weather (rain, heat) and how much water people pumped. It must tell us the water level in each . It is never allowed to look at the real level while it guesses. Think of guessing how full a bathtub is when you only know how long the tap ran and how much went down the drain. Experts call this a .

    The twist. Experts know a lot about the underground: which layer lies on which, which pump takes water from which layer, and simple rules such as "pumping lowers the water". We write that knowledge in a and give it to the computer. Then we check that the computer really uses it, like a maths teacher who wants to see the working, not just the answer. The project's title is a question: right for the right reasons?

    The four questions we want to answer

    1. Does the knowledge help the computer guess better?
    2. Do simple rules, like "more pumping can never raise the water", make its answers more believable?
    3. Does it really use the knowledge, or does it cheat with a ?
    4. Can it explain its guesses in words an expert would accept, without making things up?

    Above all four sits one big question, added after the second review: when does knowing how the underground works actually help? A clear "it helps here, not there" is a good result too.

    Next: the place and the underground

    Chapter 2

    The place and the underground

    A flat plain of vines and villages around Perpignan, between the hills and the sea, with three kinds of water layers stacked underneath.

    The Roussillon plain is in the far south of France, in the département of the Pyrénées-Orientales. Rivers come down from the mountains (the Têt, the Agly, the Tech, the Réart) and cross it to reach the Mediterranean.

    Underground, the water is not in lakes. It sits in the tiny gaps between grains of sand and gravel, or in the cracks of rock. A layer that holds water like this is an . Under the plain there are three kinds, like the layers of a cake:

    • The river gravel layer (): sand and gravel left by the rivers, just under the surface. It fills and empties quickly.
    • The deep sand-and-clay layers (the ): a thick pile of sand sheets separated by clay, from about 10 to 250 m down in our wells. It holds most of the drinking water, and reacts slowly.
    • Limestone with holes (): under the Corbières hills in the north. Water rushes through cracks and tunnels.

    These three colours mean the same layers everywhere in this guide.

    How the level is measured

    A is a thin pipe going down to one layer. Water enters at the bottom and rises in the pipe to the same height as in the layer around it. A sensor records that height every day. Nobody pumps from these wells: they only listen.

    Here is the real weekly level of a few wells, from 2005 to now. The coloured bands are the parts of time we use in different ways (chapter 4). See how the level dips every summer, falls during the 2022–2025 drought, and comes back in the recovery.

    Collecting the clues

    Before any computer model, we gathered five kinds of public data, checked them, and found three surprises.

    1. The water levels Step 4, 5 October

      From , the French public water data service. The département has 101 measuring wells. We kept the 33 with long (at least 8 years), recent (still measured in 2024) and regular records inside the plain: 20 in the deep layers, 6 in the river gravel, 6 in the karst and 1 elsewhere. Those are the .

    2. The farm fields Step 5, 5 October

      From the , the fields farmers declare each year. Within 3 km of our wells in 2023: 10,222 fields covering 14,021 hectares. Vines take 32 % of the area, orchards 19 %, olives 2 %. Between 2016 and 2023 the vines stayed about the same size.

    3. Who pumps how much Step 6a, 5 October

      From the , the register where every pumping point declares its yearly volume. Here came the first surprise: towns and farms do not drink from the same place.

    4. The map of underground layers Step 6b, 13 October

      From , we read which layer lies under each well, top to bottom (its ). We found the layer of 32 of the 33 core wells. Second surprise: in 17 pairs of nearby shallow and deep wells, the shallow water was higher than the deep water only half of the time (50.5 % of the weeks). So there is no simple rule like "the top layer is always fuller".

    5. The weather and the rivers Step 8, 13 October

      From Météo-France's : rain, and soil wetness every day, on 8 km squares (141 squares cover the département). From Hub'Eau again: river flows at 45 stations. Third surprise: there is no usable measurement of the water in the irrigation canals, so we use the canals' declared yearly volumes instead.

    The twins of Millas: shallow and deep, side by side

    At Millas, two measuring wells stand 20 m apart. One stops in the river gravel (9 m deep), the other goes down into the deep layers (109 m). Same place, same rain, two different stories.

    Next: building the big weekly table

    Chapter 4

    The big weekly table

    We put all the clues into one big table, one row per well per week, and split time into study years, a practice exam and a final exam.

    One row = one well during one week. Each row holds the water level of that week, the weather of that week and of the months before (water underground remembers), the pumping nearby, and the river flows. We chose weeks, not days: the level changes slowly, and days only add noise.

    A guess we had to make. Pumping is only declared once a year. We spread each year's total over the weeks: farm pumping follows the summer dryness, town pumping is spread more evenly. This is a , an informed guess, not a measurement. We say so wherever it matters.

    Study, practice, final exam

    A computer learns like a student. If it sees the exam answers while studying, its grade means nothing. So time is cut into parts that are never mixed:

    How dry is dry? A level of 12 m means nothing on its own. So each level is also turned into a , "how unusual for this month at this well", from "very low" to "very high", like the official drought bulletins. In the final exam, about 45 % of the weeks were "very low".

    Chapter 5

    The contest

    Before building our own model, we lined up rivals to beat. The computer-learning rivals roughly halve the mistakes of a "normal year" guess during the drought.

    A score means nothing alone: "70 out of 100" is great if everyone else gets 40, and bad if everyone gets 90. So we first ran the , from the simplest to the strongest:

    • Normal-year guess (): "the level will be as it usually is at this time of year". The floor.
    • , the hydrologists' standard tool: each cause leaves a footprint on the level over the following weeks, and the footprints are added up.
    • : many small yes/no question trees that vote.
    • : networks with a memory that read the last years of weather week by week.
    • : wells pass messages to linked neighbours. One version links wells by distance, another copies a published design (Taccari et al. 2024).
    • : a big pre-trained forecasting model. It looks at past levels, so it plays a different game, the , where it is the champion.
    • A : the average of the best ones.

    A fair contest. Every rival got the same number of tries to find its best settings (, 24 tries each, then a re-check of the best three). Every neural network was trained 5 times from different and averaged, so luck matters less. Equal tries are not equal size, though: the Taccari graph has about 230,000 adjustable numbers, the LSTM about 78,000. We say so next to the results.

    What it tells us

    • The computer-learning rivals roughly halve the mistakes of the normal-year guess in the drought. Pastas cuts them by a fifth.
    • Most of the remaining mistake is a level that stays too high or too low by a fixed amount in 2024–2025, as if something changed in the pumping that our data do not show.
    • Pumping counted over the whole plain did not help. Pumping has to be linked to the right layer and the right wells. That is exactly what the knowledge map will add.
    • The two newest rivals did not beat the plain LSTM. The EA-LSTM is the weakest network in the drought (it cuts the mistakes by a third). The Taccari graph, with pumps, rivers and rain as extra nodes, scores about the same as the simple distance graph.
    • In ("very low" to "very high", like the official bulletins), the tuned LSTM and the team spot about 3 in 4 of the drought months when the level was low, and about 1 in 6 of their "low" calls is a false alarm. Pastas spots half of them, the normal-year guess almost none.

    Is it proven? Counting neighbourhoods, not wells

    Our 33 wells are not 33 independent witnesses. Wells close to each other share the same rain, the same pumps and the same layer, so they tend to agree. On the map they fall in only 14 of 10 km. A fair test therefore counts 14 voices, not 33.

    With that fair test, the advantage of the computer models over Pastas is big but not proven at the usual 5 % level. So we report first, and say plainly that 14 neighbourhoods are not enough to prove it. One exception: the tuned Taccari graph's drought skill passes the fair test, because it does better than Pastas in almost every neighbourhood. One pass among dozens of tests is a hint, not proof; version 4 will check it. We do not change the test until it says what we would like: that would be cheating with statistics.

    First version-4 results (25 October, not final yet)

    • Saved models. New runs keep the trained model and a record of how it was made (code, data, settings, machine). A saved model can be checked again, and run on new weeks after the plan is frozen.

    What we found ourselves while checking. Choosing example wells by a fixed rule showed a flat line at Corneilla: for 413 days the record gives a depth of exactly 0.00 m, labelled "Correcte" by its operator. Wells where water really flows out record a negative depth; this one never does. It is a , not a measurement.     Fourth check-up (25 October, codex: back to the subject)

    This one did not check numbers; it checked the project against the official subject. It found a real hole: the subject is about how land use and farming affect water, and no crop information was used anywhere yet, not in the data, not in the models, not in the knowledge map. You took three decisions the same day (chapter 11):

    • Crop maps for every year 2015–2025 (the ), for the whole département, so every well's 3 km circle is covered.
    • Three questions of the knowledge map became core: crops and their water needs, satellite signs of irrigation, and explanations of real model outputs.
    • A fair test written in advance: the same models with and without a weekly "" number, plus a fake version with the crops of another well. If only the real one helps, the model uses the crop information.

    While building it we learned two things. First, many changes in the crop maps are paperwork, not fields: peaches got their own code, and much pasture was simply declared for the first time (). Second, the size is right: the crops that are almost always irrigated need about half of the water farmers declare, which fits, because declared water also covers losses. The 2020 farm census then showed that only about 1 vine in 20 is irrigated, so you decided that the crop thirst counts only the crops that are mostly watered (orchards, vegetables); the version with all vines stays as a side test.

    Fifth check-up (25 October afternoon, codex on the crop work)

    Codex looked at the crop work while it was half built, and said: finish it and lock it before running the test. Two of its warnings were real problems in the code:

    • A run could mix old and new crop numbers. If the crop file changed while a model was half trained, the finished parts could have been kept with the old numbers. Now every crop table carries a fingerprint of its numbers, and a run that sees other numbers stops.

    Next: the knowledge map

    Chapter 8

    The knowledge map

    We are now writing what experts know about the underground as a map of facts a computer can read, check and query.

    A is a set of small sentences, each made of a thing, a link and another thing. The computer can follow the links like roads, and check that nothing contradicts anything else.

    • Borrowed words. We international vocabularies (for wells, observations, time, places), like using standard LEGO bricks so that other projects can connect. We only invent the pieces that are missing, in our own small module called lulcw:.
    • A checklist of 16 questions. The map must answer questions like "which pumps may take water from the same layer as this well, how far away, and on what evidence?". These were strengthened on 24 October: each now defines the answer, its evidence, missing information and test cases. The six core questions are approved; the remaining scope is under review.
    • Knowing what we do not know. A covered layer is not necessarily confined, a nearby river is not proof of recharge, and a green field is not proof of irrigation. The questions keep recorded facts, inferred possibilities and model results distinct, so the map can explain uncertainty instead of hiding it.
    • An honest claim. The same facts written in plain tables would give the computer exactly the same links. So if the predictions improve, the credit goes to the knowledge. The map's own value is that the knowledge becomes explicit, checkable, traceable and reusable. A test will show the two give the same links.

    The pump detective (done on 23 October)

    To know which pumps can lower which wells, we must know which layer each pump drinks from. The register does not say. Of 2,137 underground pumping points, only a few have an official record. So we play detective with clues, from strongest to weakest:

    1. The official record of the borehole, when it exists. We still check it against the depth: four "river gravel" boreholes were 96 to 130 m deep, far too deep for that layer, so we moved them to the deep layer.
    2. The "connected to a river" flag in the register, combined with the layer map under the point. We measured how often each clue is right on 130 boreholes whose layer is known: "connected" means river gravel 95 % of the time; "not connected, deep layer below" means the deep layer 92 % of the time. But "not connected" alone is right only about half the time.
    3. Weaker clues: a name like "PUITS" (a dug well, so shallow), or a single layer under the point.

    Each answer gets a . A catch: all 130 boreholes used for the check are town or industry wells, none is a farm well. So we cannot measure the clues on farm wells, and those stay "possible".

    What the links can say (decided on 24 October). The main runs use the confident and probable links. They cover 72 % of the plain's declared underground pumping if we only ask for the layer family, and 62 % if we ask for the exact layer. Farm wells get 0 %: all their links are possible or unknown. So the main results will speak about tap-water pumping on the deep layers, in a plain of farms; farm pumping is tested only on the side, with all links, and marked provisional. Pumping without a sure layer is never counted as zero: every model will get it as a separate input, "pumping of unknown layer".

    A first answer. Around the Millas wells, 110 million m³ of drinking water were pumped within 5 km from 2012 to 2023. About 65 % came from the river gravel layers and about 35 % from the deep layers.

    The map is filled (done on 24 October)

    The empty map of words now holds our real facts: about 206,000 small sentences, written by a script from our tables and checked against them.

    • What is in it. The 65 measuring wells (each with its station, its series of weekly levels and the layers mapped under it), 47 underground layers, 201 map pieces with their stack of layers, all 2,187 underground pumps of the département, and 12,951 yearly volumes as the owners declared them.
    • Right family, wrong member. The "connected to a river" clue finds the river gravel family 95 % of the time, but the exact gravel layer (the Têt's or the Agly's, recent or ancient) only 63 % of the time. So the map now says both: "probably the river gravels" and "possibly this exact one". A question that needs the exact layer cannot be fooled by the family clue.
    • Guessed volumes. Most farm-well volumes are not read on a meter: 60 % of the declared farm groundwater is a flat-rate estimate (town wells: 98 % measured). The map keeps that label on every yearly volume.
    • Only facts, no shortcuts yet. The links the model will use (this layer lies on that one, these two wells share a layer, this pump may lower that well) are not written by the script. Rules inside the map will derive them next, and a separate table-based computation must find exactly the same links.

    The rules draw the links (done on 25 October)

    Written rules now run inside the map and draw the links from its facts. A second set of checks then inspects every fact and every link, the way a spell-checker reads a text.

    • Which layer lies on which. For each of the 201 map pieces, the rules read its pile of layers and write "A lies directly on B" (95 times) or "A lies above C, with B in between" (13 times). The rules never join two pieces: if A is on B in one place and B is on C in another, the map does not conclude that A is on C. In the plain, the result matches our earlier table of layer contacts exactly.
    • Neighbouring wells. 47 pairs of wells measure the same layer less than 5 km apart. Each pair keeps its distance and, when both depths are known, its depth difference. A missing depth stays missing, never zero.
    • Which pump may lower which well. 1,848 candidate links join a pump to a well 5 km away or less through a shared layer. When both are tied to the same exact layer, the link carries the rule "more pumping can only lower the level", which the model may build in. When the pump is known only by its layer family ("the river gravels"), the direction stays open, because that pump may draw from a neighbouring layer.
    • Evidence first, selection second. Each link keeps the evidence behind it. A selection policy (main runs: confident and probable) decides which links the model sees, and a link is selected only if both of its ends are. Changing the policy changes the selection, never the evidence.
    • The checker works. On the real map: no error; the only notes are the wells whose depth is unknown. In a small test map, nine mistakes slipped in on purpose (for example a link resting on an unknown layer, or two pieces chained together) were all caught.

    Chapter 9

    The smart model and its tests

    Next, a model shaped by the knowledge map, with built-in rules, and tests that try hard to catch it cheating.

    The model. It works like Pastas footprints, but the knowledge map decides which footprints exist. Each link in the map ("this pump, this well, same layer, 2 km") gets its own footprint, and the direction of each footprint is fixed: pumping can only push the level down, rain can only push it up. A small free part catches what the rules miss.

    Rules. A can be soft (the model is punished when it breaks it) or hard (the model is built so that it cannot break it: ). The second review added a catch: the rule must hold on the whole path, so the free part must not see the pumping, otherwise it could undo the rule.

    Six versions to compare

    To know which ingredient helps, we change one thing at a time (the ):

    • A: the usual inputs, no knowledge.
    • B: knowledge as extra facts about each well, no links.
    • C: knowledge + links by distance.
    • D: knowledge + links from the map. The main model.
    • E: D with its links scrambled.
    • F: random links, and everything linked to everything.

    C to F use exactly the same model, inputs, budget and random starts: only the links change (decided on 24 October). Three extra checks are fixed in advance: the model with no links at all; the wells' fixed facts shuffled between wells, to see whether the model only uses them as name tags (Heudorfer et al. 2024); and links scrambled but kept at the same distances, so that a gain from the meaning of the links is not mistaken for a gain from closeness.

    Catching a cheater

    • A practice world (): fake water levels made from our real weather and pumps with rules we choose, so we know the right answer. Some practice worlds are deliberately made with rules the model does not have, a missing cause, or wrong links, so the test is not too easy. A small one is built before the big runs, to make sure the tests can catch a cheater at all.
    • A scrambled map (): if the model does just as well with nonsense links, it was not using the map.
    • Agreement between repeats: if five trainings tell five different stories, none can be trusted.
    • Three ways of spreading pumping over the weeks: a conclusion must hold whichever guess we use.

    Explanations (February)

    The model's pushes become short sentences, for example: "this summer the level at well X fell mostly because of pumping from the same deep layer within 3 km". Each sentence is checked three ways: it describes what the model really computed (), every place it names exists in the map and is linked to the well, and the direction agrees with the rules. Real events serve as test cases: the rise of the deep layer in summer 2024 after pumping was reduced (BRGM bulletin), the 2023 drop, and a river-gravel well near a canal.
