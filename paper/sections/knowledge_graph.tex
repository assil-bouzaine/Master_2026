\section{The LULC-Water knowledge graph}\label{sec:kg}

The knowledge graph has one job in this study: to state, with its evidence, which drivers can reach
each monitoring well and in which direction each driver can move the well's head. Its scope was
fixed by sixteen competency questions (CQs; \cref{tab:cqs}), written with example answers before
any term was modelled \citep{Gruninger1995The}. Following the requirements-driven method LOT
\citep{Povedavillalon2022Lot}, each CQ that the models need became an executable test
\citep{Wisniewski2019Analysis}.

\subsection{A network of ontologies}\label{sec:kg-network}

LULC-Water is a network of small ontologies linked by alignment axioms, rather than one merged
ontology; this is the architecture of the Water Health Open Knowledge Graph \citep{Lippolis2025The}.
The release used in this study has 15 modules: the bridging module \texttt{lulcw:} (version 0.7.1),
its metadata module, four alignment modules and nine local copies of the terms reused from other
vocabularies. The local copies keep each graph release self-contained and versioned, and they
carry the attribution of vocabularies whose licence is not stated.

Groundwater features come from GroundWaterML2 \citep{Brodaric2018Enabling}. GroundWaterML2 is
published as UML and GML/XML, with no official OWL rendering, so we use the OWL classes of the
water ontology of SAWGraph \citep{SAWGraph2026Water}
and the property \texttt{tapsAquifer} of the hydro-reference ontology HyFO \citep{Hahmann2018Using}.
Observations, time, provenance, code lists and geometry use SOSA/SSN, OWL-Time, PROV-O, SKOS and
GeoSPARQL~1.1 \citep{W3C2017Ssn,Janowicz2019Sosa,W3C2017Time,W3C2013ProvO,W3C2009Skos,Car2022Geosparql};
rivers use HY\_Features \citep{OGC2018HyFeatures};
land use the INSPIRE HILUCS code list, to which the crop classes point by
\texttt{skos:broadMatch} \citep{INSPIRE2026Hilucs};
and crop codes are matched to AGROVOC \citep{Caracciolo2013The}.
SWEET \citep{Raskin2005Knowledge},
which has been extended for hydrogeology before \citep{Tripathi2008Developing}, is an alignment
target for the aquifer types.

Alignments are conservative. A module states \texttt{rdfs:subClassOf} only where the relation holds
for every instance, and \texttt{skos:closeMatch} otherwise, so that no alignment can produce a wrong
inference. An alignment ledger lists, for every mapping, the subsumptions that must hold and those
that must not, and a reasoner (HermiT; \citealp{Glimm2014Hermit})
checks both on every change. For example, \texttt{lulcw:Piezometer} is a close match of the
GroundWaterML2 well, not a subclass of it: the monitoring network includes springs, which are not
drilled wells.

\subsection{The bridging module and the influence pattern}\label{sec:kg-module}

\texttt{lulcw:} holds only what the reused vocabularies lack: 384 terms (81 classes, 101 object
properties, 96 datatype properties, 22 SKOS concept schemes and 84 controlled values), each with an
English and a French label and a definition. Three modelling rules carry most of its domain
knowledge.

\emph{Uncertain facts stay uncertain.} Most abstraction facilities have no borehole log, so the
unit a facility draws from is a qualified \texttt{UnitAttribution}: the rule that proposed it, its
rank among the candidates, its evidence, its resolution (a group of units or one exact unit) and a
confidence level. An explicit ``unknown'' is allowed, and no facility is forced into exactly
one unit. Confidence levels are measured, not assumed: a rule gives ``probable'' only for the
water uses on which its precision was measured against the 130 boreholes whose log records the
unit. Two limits are stated wherever these links are used: a geological stack under a point does
not prove a hydraulic connection, and the investigation depth of a well is not its screened depth.

\emph{Types follow written rules.} Aquifer types come from a written mapping of the BD~LISA
hydrogeological entities, not from the database's permeability label: alluvium is an
\texttt{AlluvialAquifer}, the Pliocene sands and clays a \texttt{MultilayerAquiferSystem}, karst a
\texttt{KarstAquifer} and impermeable units a \texttt{ConfiningUnit}. Geological cover, local
confinement and hydraulic exchange are kept as separate relations, and every stratigraphic
contact keeps the map polygon that witnesses it, so that a contact in one place is never chained
with a contact in another.

\emph{Causal knowledge has two levels.} At the class level, a mechanism specification states a
conditional response, for example: the head of a well does not rise when abstraction increases
from a facility attributed to the well's unit within a stated distance, other inputs held fixed.
These are qualified constraints for a model, not claims of observed causality. At the instance
level, each candidate pathway is an \texttt{Influence} node (the n-ary relation pattern) with a
source, a target, a mechanism, a sign, a distance, a confidence level, its evidence
(\texttt{prov:wasDerivedFrom}), the rule execution that produced it and a decision on whether its
sign may constrain a model (\cref{fig:influence}). Annual abstraction volumes, crop areas and demand estimates are SOSA
observations with OWL-Time intervals; weekly series stay in Parquet files that the graph
references.

\subsection{Rules, shapes and competency questions}\label{sec:kg-rules}

The facts graph (\nKgFactTriples{} triples; \cref{tab:kg-instances}) is built from the source tables
with rdflib. Derived knowledge comes from 29 SHACL-AF SPARQL rules \citep{W3C2017Shacl,W3C2017ShaclAf} run with pySHACL \citep{Sommer2026Pyshacl}
in a fixed order: stratigraphic contacts and orderings, the selection of pump-to-unit
attributions under a named policy, same-unit well links, and the influences of four mechanisms:
pumping drawdown (\nInflPumping), diffuse recharge (\nInflRecharge), river exchange (\nInflRiver) and
canal return flow (\nInflCanal). Rules are written in SPARQL because OWL property chains cannot test
a distance or a date. The rules add \nKgRuleTriples{} triples. SHACL then validates the result:
64 node shapes, with 234 property shapes and 79 SPARQL constraints, check the completeness and
the consistency of the facts and of the rule outputs. The released graph has no violation.

Each implemented CQ is a SPARQL query paired with a status query, whose answer is
``supported'', ``partial'', ``unknown'' or ``conflicting'', so that a missing
answer is never read as a negative one. A test compares the answers with an oracle computed
independently from the source tables with pandas and GeoPandas, both on small fixtures that
contain conflicting, unknown and missing cases and on the real data; negative controls, fixtures
that must fail, guard the rules. Ten CQs are implemented (CQ1--CQ8, CQ11 and CQ12). CQ10 needs the
models' results and CQ15 the satellite rule. Quality gates run on every change of the ontology:
consistency under the ELK and HermiT reasoners through ROBOT \citep{Kazakov2013The,Glimm2014Hermit,Jackson2019Robot},
the OOPS! pitfall scan \citep{Povedavillalon2014Oops} (no critical pitfall; five range axioms that
the definitions implied were added), SHACL conformance, every CQ test, and a usage audit (310 of
the 384 terms are used by the released data).

\subsection{From the graph to model inputs}\label{sec:kg-export}

The export turns the graph into two edge tables over one node inventory: \nWells{} wells, the
groundwater abstraction facilities, the weather cells, the river gauges and the plain's canal
intakes. In arm~C, the edges follow the distance rules of the Taccari-style baseline
\citep{Taccari2024Spatial}, applied to single facilities (\cref{sec:models}). In arm~D, every edge
is read from the graph through the CQ queries: pumping from the facilities attributed to the
well's unit within \SI{5}{km}, at a confidence the policy admits; recharge proxies from the
weather cells over the outcrop of the well's unit within \SI{5}{km}, weighted by each cell's share
of that outcrop; river exchange from the gauge of an associated watercourse; canal return flow
where a source documents a return; and same-unit links between wells. Where the graph holds no
link, arm~D has none: there is no distance fallback. Both arms weight an edge with the same
function of distance, so that they differ in which edges exist, not in how an edge is weighted.
Several evidence paths between one driver and one well make one edge, which is signed, and
eligible for a sign constraint, only if every path agrees. Abstraction that the policy does not
attribute to a well's unit enters as an explicit ``unattributed'' input, never as zero.

The main policy admits confident and probable attributions: \nDPumpPairs{} facility--well pairs,
from \nDPumpFacilities{} facilities to \nDPumpWells{} wells. Policies that admit confident
attributions only, or all of them, give sensitivity runs. The export records the hashes of every
file of the graph release it was built from (facts, rules, shapes and the 15 ontology modules),
so that each result can be traced to the exact graph. \Cref{tab:arm-coverage} shows what reaches
the wells in each arm: arm~D connects fewer wells than arm~C for every relation, because the graph
holds a link only where it has evidence for one. Pumping is the most affected: no irrigation
abstraction can be attributed to an exact unit, so arm~D routes only drinking-water abstraction,
and the rest enters through the unattributed input.

Finally, an equivalence test recomputes arm~D's edges from the source tables with plain pandas
and GeoPandas rules (the pumping edges, the recharge weights within $10^{-5}$ and the set of
unattributed facilities) and finds the same edges. Once its rules are known, the graph's edges
can be reproduced without the ontology. In the rest of the paper, differences between arms~D
and~C are therefore credited to the knowledge the graph carries; the ontology is credited with
making that knowledge explicit, traceable to its sources, checkable and reusable.
